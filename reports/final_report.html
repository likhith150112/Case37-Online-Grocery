1. Context and Problem Statement


Local supermarket chains face stiff competition from quick-commerce giants. While brick-and-mortar stores possess rich inventory, customer orders frequently fail due to stock-outs during peak picking hours, rigid delivery schedules, and poor handling of unavailable items. When an ordered brand of milk or rice is missing from the shelf, customer orders are either cancelled or arbitrarily replaced with unwanted items, leading to high return rates and customer frustration.


To solve this, FreshCart requires an online grocery ordering platform featuring:

1. Guaranteed Delivery Slots: Time-windowed delivery slots with real-time capacity management.

2. Intelligent Substitution Logic: An automated engine that detects out-of-stock items and recommends category-matched, price-capped alternatives while respecting customer allergen and dietary filters.

3. Explicit Customer Consent: Giving customers complete authority to accept or reject suggested replacements prior to dispatch.

    1. Prototype Specification: Substitution Logic


    The central engineering innovation in Case 37 is the Substitution Logic Engine. When a customer adds an item that is out of stock (or becomes unavailable before packing), the system must suggest an alternative based on four strict rules:

    1. Category Invariance: The candidate must belong to the exact same category (e.g., Dairy $\to$ Dairy, Staples $\to$ Staples).

    2. Price Tolerance Constraint: The candidate price increase cannot exceed $\max(\Delta_{user}, \text{Price}_{original} \times 0.20)$. Unreasonable price jumps are blocked.

    3. Allergen & Dietary Safety: If the customer specifies allergens (tree-nut, gluten, dairy, egg), any candidate matching those allergens is immediately filtered out.

    4. Preference-Weighted Ranking: In-stock candidates are scored by matching preference tags (e.g., #toned, #whole-wheat, #organic), then by lowest absolute price differential.

    3. Ethics of AI-Driven Dynamic and Personalised Pricing


    Modern online retailers frequently experiment with AI models that vary prices based on user location, browsing device (e.g. iPhone vs Android), purchasing urgency, or past order frequency. In the grocery domain, this introduces profound ethical and social hazards.


    3.1 Ethical Hazards in Essential Food Retail:

    1. Exploitation of Vulnerability: Groceries (milk, bread, rice, salt) are basic life essentials. Raising prices dynamically during bad weather, evening pick-up crunches, or targeting elderly/low-income demographics is exploitative price gouging.

    2. Surveillance Pricing & Information Asymmetry: Customers have no means of knowing whether a neighbor ordering the exact same carton of milk paid ₹10 less simply because the algorithm flagged their phone battery as low or location as affluent.

    3. Disparate Impact & Bias: Models trained on historical data often correlate neighborhood postal codes with creditworthiness or willingness to pay, resulting in algorithmic redlining.


    3.2 FreshCart Ethical AI Charter & Mitigation Policies:

  • **Rule 1: Uniform Base Pricing:** The base price of all grocery items is strictly fixed by SKU and visible to all customers regardless of device, gender, name, or order history.
  • **Rule 2: Transparent Substitution Pricing:** If a substitute item costs more than the out-of-stock item (up to 20%), the exact price differential ($\Delta \text{Price}$) must be prominently displayed in green/red font before checkout.
  • **Rule 3: Algorithmic Neutrality Audit:** All customer personal identifiers (name, gender, demographic background) are stripped from substitution and delivery recommendation pipelines (validated in Experiment L5.5).
  • **Rule 4: Human-in-the-Loop Governance:** Price ceilings and delivery fees are governed by human store managers, never autonomous black-box machine learning models.

    T3.2: Technical and Security Risks of AI-Generated Code

    • 3. Intellectual Property and Licensing Contamination:

  • If an AI was trained on copyleft code (GPL v3), it may emit verbatim snippets into a commercial codebase without appropriate attribution, creating severe legal liability.

  • During our prototype development, ChatGPT initially wrote:

    T4.2: Black-Box vs. White-Box Test Design Techniques


    1. Black-Box Testing (Specification-Based):

    Black-box testing derives test cases purely from requirements specifications without knowledge of internal code paths.


  • **Equivalence Partitioning (EP):** Inputs are divided into valid and invalid classes.
  • *Valid Partition:* Price delta $\le 20\%$ of base price (e.g. Base = ₹100, Candidate = ₹115 $\to$ ACCEPT).
  • *Invalid Partition:* Price delta $> 20\%$ of base price (e.g. Base = ₹100, Candidate = ₹125 $\to$ REJECT).
  • **Boundary Value Analysis (BVA):** Defects concentrate at boundaries between partitions.
  • For a base price of ₹100 with a $20\%$ cap (Limit = ₹120.00):
  • *Nominal Boundary:* Candidate = ₹120.00 (Boundary Pass).
  • *Above Boundary:* Candidate = ₹120.01 (Boundary Fail).
  • *Below Boundary:* Candidate = ₹119.99 (Boundary Pass).
  • **Decision Table Testing:** Maps complex combinations of conditions (Category Match, In Stock, Allergen Flag, Price $\le$ Cap) to expected substitution outputs.

    • 2. White-Box Testing (Structure-Based):

    White-box testing derives test cases from internal source code logic and control flow graphs.

  • **Statement Coverage:** Ensures every line of Python code executes at least once. (FreshCart achieved $85\%$ statement coverage).
  • **Branch / Decision Coverage:** Verifies that both True and False outcomes of every `if` condition (e.g., `if requested.in_stock:`) are tested.
  • **Path Coverage:** Ensures all independent linear paths through nested loops and conditional branches execute without uncaught exceptions.


    • T4.3: Automated vs. Manual Testing and Break-Even Analysis


    While manual testing is essential for exploratory, visual, and heuristic assessments, automated testing provides unmatched regression speed and repeatability.


    Mathematical Break-Even Analysis Formula:

    $$N = \frac{\text{Initial Automation Setup Time}}{\text{Manual Test Execution Time} - \text{Automated Test Execution Time}}$$


    FreshCart Empirical Measurement Data (10 Test Cases, 5 Repetitions):

  • Manual execution time per full cycle ($T_{manual}$): **36.5 minutes** (browsing, typing inputs, verifying screens).
  • Automated Selenium execution time per full cycle ($T_{auto}$): **3.0 minutes** (runs headlessly in background).
  • Initial setup & scripting time ($T_{setup}$): **90.0 minutes**.
  • Time saved per test cycle: $36.5 - 3.0 = \mathbf{33.5\text{ minutes}}$.
  • **Break-Even Point ($N$):**

    • $$N = \frac{90.0}{33.5} \approx \mathbf{2.69\text{ test cycles}}$$


    Conclusion: After just 3 regression cycles, automated testing fully recovers its development investment and continues to save 33.5 minutes on every subsequent release.

    T5.4: Software Reliability Engineering, MTBF/MTTR, and Availability


    1. Core Reliability Formulas:

  • **Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF):** The average operational uptime between consecutive system crashes:

    • $$\text{MTBF} = \frac{\text{Total Operational Uptime}}{\text{Number of Failures}}$$

  • **Mean Time to Repair (MTTR):** The average time required to diagnose, patch, and restore the service:

    • $$\text{MTTR} = \frac{\text{Total Downtime}}{\text{Number of Failures}}$$

  • **System Availability ($A$):**

    • $$A = \frac{\text{MTBF}}{\text{MTBF} + \text{MTTR}} \times 100\%$$


    Application in FreshCart:

    If the online grocery backend runs continuously for 30 days ($720\text{ hours}$) and experiences two brief service restarts lasting 15 minutes each ($0.5\text{ hours}$ total downtime):

    $$\text{MTBF} = \frac{719.5}{2} = 359.75\text{ hours}, \quad \text{MTTR} = \frac{0.5}{2} = 0.25\text{ hours} \ (15\text{ mins})$$

    $$A = \frac{359.75}{359.75 + 0.25} = \frac{359.75}{360.00} = \mathbf{99.93\% \ (Three Nines)}$$



    T5.5: Ethical AI Engineering: Demographic Fairness, Transparency, and Algorithmic Bias


    1. Algorithmic Discrimination in Retail:

    AI recommendation and pricing algorithms frequently encode societal biases:

  • **Price Discrimination:** Models might quote higher prices to users in specific postal codes or using specific devices (e.g. higher prices for iOS users). In basic food retail, this constitutes unethical price gouging.
  • **Demographic Bias:** Algorithms trained on past purchase volumes might prioritize luxury items over basic staples for certain customer names or demographic groups.

    • 2. FreshCart Ethical Engineering Safeguards:

    1. Feature Blinding: Sensitive customer attributes (name, gender, demographic labels) are strictly excluded from the substitution ranking algorithm. The function suggest_substitution accepts only item category, price, in-stock status, and explicit dietary flags.

    2. Fairness Testing (Experiment L5.5): Synthetic user profiles varying only in name (*Aarav* vs *Ananya* vs *Zaid*) or gender are passed through the substitution engine. The system guarantees $100\%$ identical candidate rankings and prices across all demographic profiles.

    3. Transparent Price Deltas: Any price variation between the original and substitute product is explicitly displayed with green/red badges before payment confirmation.
